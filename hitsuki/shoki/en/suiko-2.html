Nihon Shoki, Book 22 — Empress Suiko (part 2) · page 39 of 50

Empress Suiko, the Envoys to the Great Tang, and the Death of the Crown Prince of the Upper Palace

推古天皇と大唐への使い・上宮太子の死

The 13th to the 36th years of Empress Suiko record the completion of the jōroku (sixteen-foot) Buddha images, the dispatch of Ono no Imoko to the Great Tang and the arrival of Pei Shiqing, negotiations with Silla and Mimana, and the compilation of the Tennōki (Record of the Emperors) and Kokki (Record of the Country). After Prince Umayado no Toyotomimi and the Great Minister of the Island (嶋大臣) passed away and the offices of Sōjō and Sōzu were established, the empress left words to Prince Tamura and Prince Yamashiro no Ōe and died.

Key points to remember

  1. Main textIn the 7th month of the 15th year, Ono no Omi Imoko, of the rank Greater Propriety, was sent to the Great Tang, and in the 4th month of the 16th year the Tang envoy Pei Shiqing came to Tsukushi together with Imoko. Imoko reported that the letter of the Tang emperor had been stolen from him in Paekche; the ministers said he should be exiled, but the empress pardoned him.
  2. Main textIn the 9th month of the 16th year, when Pei Shiqing returned home, Imoko was again sent with him as ambassador, and the empress sent a letter beginning "東天皇敬白西皇帝". At this time eight students and student monks in all were also sent to Tang.
  3. Main textIn the 2nd month of the 29th year, Prince Umayado no Toyotomimi passed away at the Ikaruga Palace, and people lamented, "The sun and moon have lost their light, and heaven and earth have crumbled." It is written that the Koguryŏ monk Eji vowed that he would surely die on the 5th day of the 2nd month of the following year and meet the Crown Prince in the Pure Land, and that he died on that day.
  4. Main textIn the 28th year, the Crown Prince and the Great Minister of the Island deliberated together and compiled the Tennōki and Kokki, as well as the fundamental records (本記) of the Omi, Muraji, Tomo-no-Miyatsuko, Kuni-no-Miyatsuko, the hundred and eighty be (部) and the common people.
  5. Main textIn the 32nd year, prompted by an incident in which a monk struck his grandfather with an axe, Kanroku was made Sōjō and 鞍部徳積* was made Sōzu to supervise the monks and nuns. A survey in the 9th month recorded forty-six temples, 816 monks and 569 nuns.
  6. Compared with the KojikiFor Empress Suiko, the Kojiki records that she ruled the realm for thirty-seven years and that her tomb was at 大野岡上* and was later moved to the 科長大陵*. In the Nihon Shoki, the empress died in the 3rd month of the 36th year and, in accordance with her last edict, was buried in the tomb of Prince Takeda.

What the main text says

In the 4th month of the 13th year, the empress, together with the Crown Prince, the Great Minister, the princes and the ministers, made a vow and began making two jōroku (sixteen-foot) Buddha images, one of bronze and one of embroidery, and Kuratsukuri no Tori was made the craftsman for the images. Hearing of this, 大興王*, king of the country of Koguryŏ, sent three hundred ryō of gold as tribute. In the 10th month, the Crown Prince took up residence at the Ikaruga Palace. In the 4th month of the 14th year, the jōroku bronze image was completed and was to be placed in the golden hall of Gangō-ji, but the image was taller than the door of the hall; when the workmen discussed breaking the door, Tori's excellent skill made it possible to bring it in without breaking the door. From this year, Buddhist feasts came to be held at every temple on the 8th day of the 4th month and the 15th day of the 7th month. In the 5th month, citing the services of his grandfather Shiba Tatto, his father 多須那* and his aunt 嶋女*, and his present service as well, the empress gave Tori the rank of Greater Benevolence and twenty chō of paddy fields in 坂田郡*, Ōmi Province, and Tori built 金剛寺* on those fields for the empress.

In the 7th month of the 14th year, at the empress's request, the Crown Prince lectured on the Śrīmālā Sutra over three days, and in this year he also lectured on the Lotus Sutra at the 岡本宮* palace, so the empress gave the Crown Prince one hundred chō of paddy fields in Harima Province, which the Crown Prince donated to Ikaruga-dera. In the 2nd month of the 15th year, the 壬生部* was established, and, citing how the emperors of her ancestral line had revered the gods of heaven and earth and worshiped the mountains and rivers, the empress issued an edict that the ministers should wholeheartedly worship the gods of heaven and earth. Some days later, the Crown Prince and the Great Minister led the officials in worshiping the gods of heaven and earth. In the 7th month, Ono no Omi Imoko, of the rank Greater Propriety, was sent to the Great Tang, with 鞍作福利* as interpreter. In the winter of this year, the 高市池*, 藤原池* and other ponds were made in the province of Yamato (倭国), a great canal was dug at 栗隈* in Yamashiro Province, and miyake estates were established in each province.

In the 4th month of the 16th year, Imoko returned from the Great Tang, and the Tang envoy Pei Shiqing and twelve attendants arrived in Tsukushi following Imoko. It is recorded that in Tang, Imoko was called 蘇因高*. In the 6th month, the envoys arrived at the port of Naniwa and were lodged in a new guesthouse, but Imoko reported that the letter given him by the Tang emperor had been stolen from him in Paekche, and the ministers said he should be exiled. The empress pardoned Imoko, saying it would not be good if this were heard by the guests from a great country. In the 8th month, Pei Shiqing presented the Tang emperor's letter at court; the princes, royal relatives and ministers wore gold hair ornaments (髻華) on their heads and garments of brocade, purple, embroidery and the like. In the 9th month, when Pei Shiqing returned home, Imoko was sent with him as ambassador and 吉士雄成* as vice-envoy; the empress sent a letter saying, "The Emperor of the East respectfully addresses the Emperor of the West," and eight students and student monks were also sent.

In the 4th month of the 17th year, ten Paekche monks including 道欣* and seventy-five lay people, who had met a storm on their way to the land of Wu (呉国), arrived at the port of 葦北津* in Higo Province; while they were being sent back, eleven of the monks asked to stay and lived at Gangō-ji. In the 9th month, Imoko returned from the Great Tang, but the interpreter 福利* did not return. In the 3rd month of the 18th year, the king of Koguryŏ presented the monks Donchō and 法定*; Donchō knew the Five Classics and was also able to make pigments, paper and ink, and water mills (碾磑). In the 10th month, envoys from Silla and Mimana arrived at the capital and stated the purpose of their mission at court, and the Great Minister stood up to listen. In the 2nd month of the 20th year, the Imperial Lady Dowager (皇太夫人) 堅塩媛* was reburied in the 檜隈大陵* tomb and eulogies were offered; in this year 路子工*, who had come from Paekche, built the form of Mount Sumeru and a Wu-style bridge (呉橋) in the southern courtyard, and Mimashi transmitted the gigaku dance.

In the 12th month of the 21st year, at 片岡* the Crown Prince gave food and drink to a starving man lying by the road, and took off his own garment and laid it over him. After the man died and was buried, the Crown Prince, saying that he had been no ordinary person, had the grave opened; there was no corpse, and only the garment lay folded on top of the coffin, so the people of the time said that it is truly so that a sage knows a sage. In the 6th month of the 22nd year, Inukami no Kimi Mitasuki and 矢田部造* were sent to the Great Tang, and the two returned in the 9th month of the 23rd year. In the 11th month of the 23rd year, the Koguryŏ monk Eji returned to his country. In the 8th month of the 26th year, Koguryŏ reported that it had defeated an army of three hundred thousand of Emperor Yang of Sui and presented captives, drums and wind instruments, and other things. In the 28th year, the top of the 檜隈陵* tomb was covered with gravel, and the Crown Prince and the Great Minister of the Island compiled the Tennōki, the Kokki and other records.

In the 2nd month of the 29th year, Prince Umayado no Toyotomimi passed away at midnight at the Ikaruga Palace; the old grieved as if they had lost a beloved child, and the young as if they had lost a loving father and mother. The prince was buried in the 磯長陵* tomb, and Eji of Koguryŏ held a Buddhist feast and vowed that he would surely die on the 5th day of the 2nd month of the following year and meet the Crown Prince in the Pure Land, and he died on that day. In the 31st year, because Silla attacked Mimana, a large army under 境部臣雄摩侶* and others was sent out without waiting for the envoys' reply; Silla asked to surrender, but the Great Minister regretted having sent the army so early. In the 4th month of the 32nd year, an incident occurred in which a monk struck his grandfather with an axe; on Kanroku's memorial, all but the wicked monk were pardoned, and Kanroku was made Sōjō and 鞍部徳積* Sōzu to supervise the monks and nuns. In the 10th month, the Great Minister asked to have 葛城県* made his own fief, but the empress refused, saying that later generations would say a foolish woman had ruled the realm and lost that district. In the 5th month of the 34th year, the Great Minister, son of Iname no Sukune, passed away; it is recorded that he was called the Great Minister of the Island (嶋大臣) because he had built a small island in the pond in the garden of his house beside the Asuka River.

In the 34th year, long rains continued from the 3rd month to the 7th month, the realm suffered a great famine, and robbery and theft occurred frequently. In the 2nd month of the 36th year, the empress fell ill, and in the 3rd month there was a solar eclipse. The empress summoned Prince Tamura and admonished him not to speak lightly about the heavenly throne, and she instructed Prince Yamashiro no Ōe that, since he was still young, even if he desired it in his heart he should not voice it but should wait for the words of the ministers. The next day the empress died at the age of seventy-five and was given temporary enshrinement in the southern courtyard. In the 9th month the funeral rites began, and, in accordance with her last edict that, because the five grains had not ripened and the people were starving, no tomb should be built for a lavish burial and she should instead be buried in the tomb of Prince Takeda, she was buried in the tomb of Prince Takeda.

A plain retelling of what the main text of the Nihon Shoki says (no published translation has been used).

Variant accounts

After its main text, the Nihon Shoki often sets out other versions, introduced with words such as "a certain book says" (一書曰).

One version says (一云)

Concerning the attire on the day Pei Shiqing was received, the main text says that brocade, purple, embroidery, woven fabric and figured silks of five colors were used for the garments, but one version says that the colors of the garments all followed the colors of the caps.

In the original text

東天皇敬白西皇帝

The Emperor of the East respectfully addresses the Emperor of the West (the opening of the letter sent to the Tang emperor).

日月失輝，天地既崩

The sun and the moon have lost their light, and heaven and earth have crumbled (words of the people lamenting the Crown Prince's death).

聖之知聖，其實哉

It is true that a sage knows a sage.

愚癡婦人臨天下，以頓亡其縣

A foolish woman ruled the realm and promptly lost that district (the judgment of later generations that the empress feared).

Deities and people, and the shrines that enshrine them

  • 豊御食炊屋姫天皇*Empress Suiko. She died at seventy-five in the 3rd month of the 36th year and was buried in the tomb of Prince Takeda.
  • Prince Umayado no Toyotomimi (廐户豐聰耳皇子)The Crown Prince of the Upper Palace (上宮太子). He lectured on the Śrīmālā Sutra and the Lotus Sutra, compiled the Tennōki and Kokki, and passed away at the Ikaruga Palace in the 29th year.
  • The Great Minister of the Island (嶋大臣)Great Minister. Son of Iname no Sukune. He compiled the Kokki and other records together with the Crown Prince, and passed away in the 34th year.
  • Kuratsukuri no Tori (鞍作鳥)The craftsman of the jōroku Buddha images. He brought the image into the golden hall without breaking the door, and was granted the rank of Greater Benevolence.
  • Ono no Omi Imoko (小野臣妹子)Of the rank Greater Propriety. He was sent to the Great Tang and in Tang was called 蘇因高*.
  • Pei Shiqing (裴世清)An attendant for guests (掌客) of the Tang office 鴻臚寺*. He came to the court together with Imoko and presented the Tang emperor's letter.
  • Inukami no Kimi Mitasuki (犬上君御田鍬)He was sent to the Great Tang in the 22nd year and returned the following year.
  • Donchō (曇徴)A monk of Koguryŏ. He knew the Five Classics and made pigments, paper and ink, and water mills (碾磑).
  • Eji (慧慈)A monk of Koguryŏ and the Crown Prince's teacher. It is written that after returning to his country he heard of the Crown Prince's death and died on that day the following year.
  • Kanroku (觀勒)A monk of Paekche. He submitted a memorial asking for the pardon of the monks and nuns, and became the first Sōjō.
  • Prince Tamura (田村皇子)The prince whom the empress, on her sickbed, admonished not to speak lightly about the heavenly throne.
  • Prince Yamashiro no Ōe (山背大兄)The person whom the empress, on her sickbed, instructed to wait for the words of the ministers.

In the Kojiki

Kojiki, Part 3, final section — section on Empress Suiko (豐御食炊屋比賣命*)

The Kojiki records her reign as thirty-seven years. The Nihon Shoki says that the empress died at seventy-five in the 3rd month of the 36th year.

坐小治田宮。治天下參拾漆歲
Kojiki, Part 3 — end of the section on Empress Suiko (tomb)

The Kojiki places her tomb at 大野岡上* and records that it was later moved to the 科長大陵*. The Nihon Shoki says that, in accordance with her last edict, she was buried in the tomb of Prince Takeda.

御陵在大野岡上。後遷科長大陵也
Kojiki, Part 3 — section on Empress Suiko (the last entry of the Kojiki)

The section on Empress Suiko in the Kojiki ends with only her palace, the length of her reign and her tomb; for the envoys to the Great Tang, the arrival of Pei Shiqing, and the death of the Crown Prince of the Upper Palace, no corresponding entries could be confirmed in the Kojiki.
