The Song of the Wild Goose’s Eggs and the Ship 枯野*

雁の卵の歌と枯野の船

The emperor asked Takeuchi-no-Sukune-no-Mikoto in a song about a wild goose laying eggs on 日女嶋*, and Takeuchi-no-Sukune answered in a song. In this reign a fast ship, “枯野”*, was made from a huge tree, and from the ship’s charred remains a koto (zither) was made whose sound carried for seven ri.

Key points to remember

  1. Main textIn a song, the emperor asked Takeuchi-no-Sukune-no-Mikoto (建内宿禰) about a wild goose having laid eggs on 日女嶋*, and Takeuchi-no-Sukune answered in a song. The song he then sang after being granted the emperor’s koto is described as “the 片歌 of the 本岐歌” (readings unconfirmed).
  2. Main textThe tall tree west of the 免寸河 river* was so large that its shadow in the morning sun reached Awaji Island (淡道嶋) and its shadow in the evening sun crossed Mount 高安山*. The fast ship made from this tree was “枯野”*, and morning and evening it carried cold spring water from Awaji Island as drinking water for the emperor (大御水).
  3. Main textWhen 枯野* broke down, it was used as fuel for making salt, and a koto made from its charred remains had a sound that carried for seven ri. The song sung then is described as “the 歌返 of the 志都歌” (readings unconfirmed).
  4. Main textIt is recorded that Emperor Nintoku lived to the age of eighty-three and that his tomb is at 耳原 in Mozu (毛受之耳原)*.
  5. Compared with the Nihon ShokiIn the Nihon Shoki, the wild goose laid its eggs in the 3rd month of the 50th year, at the 茨田堤 embankment* in Kawachi, and the emperor asks Takeuchi-no-Sukune-no-Mikoto (武内宿禰) in a song after sending a messenger to confirm it. There is no scene in which he is granted the koto and sings a 片歌. The story of the ship 枯野 and the koto could not be confirmed in Book 11; instead there is an entry for the 62nd year in which a ship is ordered to be built from a great tree in Tōtōmi.
  6. CommentaryKokugakuin University’s database of deity names, touching on the view that Takamimusubi-no-Kami (高御産巣日神, also called 高木神) was originally a sun deity, notes that a connection between huge trees and the sun can be seen in the huge-tree legend of the “枯野 ship” in the section on Emperor Nintoku. [source, Japanese]

The story

Once, the emperor went to 日女嶋* intending to hold a banquet (豊楽). On that island a wild goose laid eggs. The emperor therefore summoned Takeuchi-no-Sukune-no-Mikoto (建内宿禰命) and asked him in a song about the wild goose laying eggs.

Takeuchi-no-Sukune answered in a song, and then, having been granted the emperor’s koto, sang a song. The text records that this song is “the 片歌 of the 本岐歌” (readings unconfirmed).

In this reign there was a single tall tree west of the 免寸河 river*. When the morning sun struck it, its shadow reached as far as Awaji Island (淡道嶋), and when the evening sun struck it, its shadow crossed Mount 高安山*. So this tree was cut down and made into a ship, and it became a ship that sailed very fast. The ship was named “枯野”*, and with it, morning and evening, cold spring water from Awaji Island was drawn and offered as drinking water for the emperor (大御水).

When this ship broke down, it was burned to make salt, and a koto was made from the wood left unburned. Its sound carried as far as seven ri. A song was sung about this, and the text records that this song is “the 歌返 of the 志都歌” (readings unconfirmed).

The emperor lived to the age of eighty-three, and his tomb is at 耳原 in Mozu (毛受之耳原)*.

A plain retelling of what the original text of the Kojiki says (no published translation has been used).

In the original text

於其嶋雁生卵

On that island a wild goose laid eggs.

其樹之影。當旦日者。逮淡道嶋。當夕日者。越高安山

When the morning sun struck it, the tree’s shadow reached Awaji Island (淡道嶋), and when the evening sun struck it, the shadow crossed Mount 高安山*.

時號其船。謂枯野

The ship was named “枯野”*.

作琴。其音響七里

When a koto was made, its sound carried for seven ri.

In the Nihon Shoki

Nihon Shoki, Book 11, the 50th year of Emperor Nintoku, spring, 3rd month (壬辰朔丙申)

In the Nihon Shoki, a person from Kawachi reports that a wild goose has laid eggs at the 茨田堤 embankment*, and after sending a messenger to confirm it, the emperor asks Takeuchi-no-Sukune-no-Mikoto (武内宿禰) in a song. The place differs from the Kojiki’s 日女嶋*, and there is no 片歌 sung by Takeuchi-no-Sukune after being granted the koto.

於茨田堤鴈産之
Nihon Shoki, Book 11, the 62nd year of Emperor Nintoku, summer, 5th month

The story of the ship 枯野* and the koto could not be confirmed in Book 11 of the Nihon Shoki. Instead, there is an entry in which 倭直吾子篭* is ordered to build a ship from a great tree that had drifted down the 大井河 river* in Tōtōmi Province, and to bring it to the port of Naniwa (難波津).

有大樹。自大井河流之停于河曲
Nihon Shoki, Book 11, the 87th year of Emperor Nintoku, winter, 10th month (癸未朔己丑)

In the Nihon Shoki, the emperor dies in the 1st month of the 87th year and is buried in the 10th month of the same year in the 百舌鳥野 tomb*; his age is not recorded. The Kojiki gives his age as eighty-three and places his tomb at 毛受之耳原*.
