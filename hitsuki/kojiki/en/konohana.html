Kojiki, Part 1 · story 15 of 17

Konohanasakuya-hime and Iwanagahime

木花之佐久夜毘売と石長比売

Ninigi-no-Mikoto, having come down from heaven, took as his wife Konohanasakuya-hime, daughter of Ōyamatsumi-no-Kami, at the cape of 笠紗*. But he sent back her elder sister Iwanagahime (石長比売), who had been offered together with her, as ugly, and the Kojiki says that this is why the lives of the emperors are not long. Sakuya-hime, suspected because she had conceived in a single night, gave birth to three children, Hoderi-no-Mikoto, Hosuseri-no-Mikoto and 火遠理命*, inside a birth hall that had been set on fire.

Read in the Nihon Shoki: The Pacification of the Central Land of Reed Plains and the Descent of the Heavenly Grandson (Section 9) ›

Key points to remember

  1. Main textBecause Ninigi-no-Mikoto sent back the elder sister Iwanagahime as ugly and took only the younger sister Konohanasakuya-hime as his wife, the lives of the children of the heavenly deity became as fleeting as the blossoms of trees, just as Ōyamatsumi-no-Kami had said. The Kojiki says that this is why, even now, the lives of the emperors are not long.
  2. Main textKonohanasakuya-hime, suspected by Ninigi-no-Mikoto because she had conceived in a single night, said, “If it is the child of the heavenly deity, it will be born safely,” entered a hall with no door, set it on fire and gave birth, bearing three children: Hoderi-no-Mikoto, Hosuseri-no-Mikoto and 火遠理命*.
  3. Main textOf the three born in the fire, the eldest, Hoderi-no-Mikoto, is noted in a small note as the ancestor of the 阿多君* of the Hayato, and the youngest, 火遠理命 * (also called 天津日高日子穂穂手見命), becomes the hero of the story of Umisachi and Yamasachi (海幸山幸) that follows.
  4. Compared with the Nihon ShokiIn variant account 2 of the Nihon Shoki, section 9, the shortening of life is attributed not to the father's vow but to the curse spoken by Iwanagahime (磐長姫) herself in her shame, and within it a “one version says” (一云) tells it as the origin of the fleeting lives not only of the emperors but of all people in the world.
  5. CommentaryAccording to the Kokugakuin University database of deity names, this story, in which the stone is rejected and a short life is received, is regarded as belonging to a type of myth called the “Banana type,” found in Southeast Asia and elsewhere, and it is thought that a story originally about the origin of death for humans in general became attached to the myth of Ninigi-no-Mikoto. [source, Japanese] [source, Japanese]
  6. CommentaryThe same database introduces the view that, through Ninigi-no-Mikoto marrying the daughter of a mountain deity and his son 火遠理命* marrying the daughter of a sea deity, the descendants of the heavenly deity take in the powers of both mountain and sea, which carries the meaning that they gain the qualification to rule the earth. [source, Japanese]

The story

天津日高日子番能邇邇藝能命 (Ninigi-no-Mikoto) met a beautiful woman at the cape of 笠紗*. When he asked whose daughter she was, she answered that she was a daughter of Ōyamatsumi-no-Kami, named Kamuatatsu-hime (神阿多都比売), also called Konohanasakuya-hime. When he asked whether she had sisters, she answered that she had an elder sister, Iwanagahime (石長比売). When Ninigi-no-Mikoto said he wished to take her as his wife, Sakuya-hime said, “I cannot answer on my own. My father, Ōyamatsumi-no-Kami, will answer.”

So he sent a messenger to her father Ōyamatsumi-no-Kami, who was greatly pleased and offered her together with her elder sister Iwanagahime, sending them with many goods laden on stands. But because the elder sister was very ugly, Ninigi-no-Mikoto was frightened when he saw her and sent her back. He kept only the younger sister, Konohanasakuya-hime, and spent one night with her.

Ōyamatsumi-no-Kami, deeply ashamed that Iwanagahime had been sent back, sent word as follows. He had offered his two daughters together because he had sworn a vow: if Iwanagahime were kept at his side, the life of the child of the heavenly deity would remain unmoving forever like a rock, even when snow falls and wind blows; and if Konohanasakuya-hime were kept at his side, he would flourish as the blossoms of trees bloom. Since he had sent back Iwanagahime and kept only Sakuya-hime, the life of the child of the heavenly deity would be as fleeting as the blossoms of trees. The Kojiki records that for this reason, down to the present, the lives of the emperors are not long.

Later Sakuya-hime came and said, “I am with child, and the birth is near. Since it is the child of the heavenly deity, I cannot give birth to it by myself as I please.” Ninigi-no-Mikoto then said, “You conceived in a single night? That is not my child. Surely it is the child of an earthly deity.” Sakuya-hime answered, “If the child I carry is the child of an earthly deity, it will not be born safely. If it is the child of the heavenly deity, it will be born safely.”

Sakuya-hime built a large hall with no doorway (無戸八尋殿), went inside, and sealed it up with clay. Then, at the time of the birth, she set fire to the hall and gave birth. The child born when the fire was blazing fiercely was Hoderi-no-Mikoto (a small note says he is the ancestor of the 阿多君* of the Hayato), next Hosuseri-no-Mikoto, and next 火遠理命*, whose other name is 天津日高日子穂穂手見命 (Hikohohodemi).

A plain retelling of what the original text of the Kojiki says (no published translation has been used).

In the original text

唯留其弟木花之佐久夜毗賣以一宿為婚

(Sending back the elder sister,) he kept only the younger sister, Konohanasakuya-hime, and spent one night with her.

雖雪零風吹，恒如石而常堅不動坐

(If Iwanagahime were kept at his side, the life of the child of the heavenly deity) would always remain firm and unmoving like a rock, even when snow falls and wind blows.

故是以至于今，天皇命等之御命不長也。

For this reason, down to the present, the lives of the emperors are not long.

若天神之御子者，幸。

If (the child I carry) is the child of the heavenly deity, it will be born safely.

In the Nihon Shoki

Nihon Shoki, Book 2, section 9, variant account 2

Variant account 2 of section 9 also has the story of the elder sister Iwanagahime (磐長姫) being rejected as ugly, but the shortening of life is attributed to Iwanagahime's curse in her shame, and its “one version says” (一云) makes it the origin of the fleeting lives of people in general (“顯見蒼生”). In the Kojiki it results from the vow of the father, Ōyamatsumi-no-Kami, and it is a story about the lives of the emperors.

故磐長姫大慙而詛之曰
Nihon Shoki, Book 2, section 9, main text

Iwanagahime does not appear in the main text of section 9; the wife's name is 鹿葦津姫*, and a small note gives her other names as 神吾田津姫* and Konohanasakuya-hime (木花之開耶姫). The points that she is suspected for conceiving in a single night and gives birth after setting fire to a doorless chamber (無戸室) are shared with the Kojiki.

名曰鹿葦津姫
Nihon Shoki, Book 2, section 9, main text

In the main text of section 9, the children born are three: Honosusori-no-Mikoto (火闌降命), Hikohohodemi-no-Mikoto (彦火火出見尊) and Hoakari-no-Mikoto (火明命), and Honosusori-no-Mikoto, born first, is noted in a small note as the first ancestor of the Hayato and others. In the Kojiki, the ancestor of the 阿多君* of the Hayato is Hoderi-no-Mikoto, and the third child is 火遠理命*.
