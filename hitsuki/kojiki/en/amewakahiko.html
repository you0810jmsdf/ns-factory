Kojiki, Part 1 · story 12 of 17

Ame-no-Wakahiko (天若日子)

天若日子

Ame-no-Wakahiko (天若日子), sent from Takamanohara to bring the Central Land of the Reed Plains (葦原中国) into submission, married a daughter of Ōkuninushi-no-Kami and made no report for eight years. Because he shot the pheasant that came to question him, he died by that very arrow when 高木神* threw it back. Ajisukitakahikone-no-Kami, who came to the funeral, was mistaken for the dead man, flew into a rage and cut down the mourning house.

Read in the Nihon Shoki: The Pacification of the Central Land of Reed Plains and the Descent of the Heavenly Grandson (Section 9) ›

Key points to remember

  1. Main textAs envoys to bring the Central Land of the Reed Plains into submission, Ame-no-Hohi-no-Kami (天菩比神) was sent first, but he fawned on Ōkuninushi-no-Kami and made no report for three years. Ame-no-Wakahiko, who was sent next, also married Shitateru-hime (下照比売), tried to make the land his own, and made no report for eight years.
  2. Main textOn the advice of Ame-no-Sagume (天佐具売), Ame-no-Wakahiko shot dead the pheasant Nakime (鳴女), who had come to ask him why. 高木神* threw that arrow back, saying “If he has an evil heart, let it strike him,” and the arrow struck Ame-no-Wakahiko in the chest as he lay asleep, and he died. The Kojiki presents this as the origin of the “returning arrow” (還矢) and of the “pheasant's one-way errand” (雉之頓使).
  3. Main textAjisukitakahikone-no-Kami, who came to the funeral, looked exactly like Ame-no-Wakahiko and was mistaken for the dead man. In his anger he cut down the mourning house with his ten-fist sword (十掬剣) and kicked it away. The Kojiki explains that this became 喪山 (the Mourning Mountain)* at the upper reaches of the 藍見河* in the land of Mino.
  4. Main textTo make known the name of Ajisukitakahikone-no-Kami as he flew away, his younger sister 高比売命* composed a song, and the Kojiki calls this song a 夷振* song.
  5. Compared with the Nihon ShokiIn the main text of the Nihon Shoki, section 9, Ame-no-Wakahiko's father 天国玉* sends a gale to carry the body up to heaven, and he is mourned in a mourning house built in heaven. In the Kojiki, his father, wife and children come down to the earth and build the mourning house on the spot.
  6. CommentaryAccording to the Kokugakuin University database of deity names, because the story of Ame-no-Wakahiko strays from the main thread of the ceding of the land, it is thought to have originally been transmitted separately from the land-ceding story. Views on the reading of his name are also divided, with readings such as Amewakahiko and Ame-no-Wakahiko. [source, Japanese]

The story

Amaterasu-Ōmikami declared that the Land of Abundant Reed Plains and Fresh Rice Ears (豊葦原水穂国) was a land to be ruled by her child Ame-no-Oshihomimi-no-Mikoto, and she sought to send him down from heaven. But Ame-no-Oshihomimi-no-Mikoto looked down from the Floating Bridge of Heaven (天浮橋), said “伊多久佐夜藝弖有那理” (it seems to be in great uproar), turned back and reported this to Amaterasu-Ōmikami. Takamimusubi-no-Kami and Amaterasu-Ōmikami then gathered the eight hundred myriad deities on the riverbed of the Heavenly River Yasu (天安河) and had Omoikane-no-Kami (思金神) think the matter over. Since there were many violent earthly deities on the earth, they discussed which deity to send to subdue them, and decided to send Ame-no-Hohi-no-Kami (天菩比神). However, Ame-no-Hohi-no-Kami fawned on Ōkuninushi-no-Kami and submitted to him, and even after three years he made no report.

Next, on the advice of Omoikane-no-Kami, they gave Ame-no-Wakahiko (天若日子), the son of Amatsukunitama-no-Kami (天津国玉神), the bow 天之麻迦古弓 and the arrows 天之波波矢 (readings unconfirmed), and sent him off. When Ame-no-Wakahiko came down to the earth, he married Shitateru-hime (下照比売), a daughter of Ōkuninushi-no-Kami, plotted to obtain the land for himself, and even after eight years he made no report. When Amaterasu-Ōmikami and Takamimusubi-no-Kami discussed whom to send to ask why, the deities recommended sending a pheasant named Nakime (鳴女).

Nakime perched on the sacred katsura tree (湯津楓) at the gate of Ame-no-Wakahiko's house and delivered the words of the heavenly deities exactly as given. Ame-no-Sagume (天佐具売), hearing this, urged him, “This bird's cry is very bad, so you should shoot it dead,” and Ame-no-Wakahiko shot the pheasant dead with the bow and arrows he had received from the heavenly deities. The arrow pierced the pheasant's breast, rose all the way to heaven, and reached Amaterasu-Ōmikami and 高木神 * (another name of Takamimusubi-no-Kami), who were on the riverbed of the Heavenly River Yasu. 高木神 saw that the bloodied arrow was the one given to Ame-no-Wakahiko, and threw it back, saying, “If he has kept his orders and shot at evil deities, let it not strike him. If he has an evil heart, let it strike him.” The arrow struck Ame-no-Wakahiko in the chest as he lay in his bed in the morning, and he died. The Kojiki records that this was the origin of the “returning arrow” (還矢), and that because the pheasant did not return, the proverb “the pheasant's one-way errand” (雉之頓使) came into being.

The weeping of Shitateru-hime, who had lost her husband, reached all the way to heaven on the wind. Ame-no-Wakahiko's father Amatsukunitama-no-Kami, who was in heaven, and Ame-no-Wakahiko's wife and children heard it, came down, and built a mourning house on that spot. They assigned the duties of the funeral to the river goose, the heron, the kingfisher, the sparrow and the pheasant, and mourned him with eight days and eight nights of ritual music and dance.

Then Ajisukitakahikone-no-Kami came to offer condolences. Because he looked exactly like Ame-no-Wakahiko, Ame-no-Wakahiko's father and wife clung to his hands and feet and wept, saying “My child was not dead!” and “My lord was not dead!” Ajisukitakahikone-no-Kami was furious, saying, “I came to mourn only because he was a dear friend. Why do you compare me to an unclean dead man?” He cut down the mourning house with the ten-fist sword (十掬剣) at his waist and kicked it away with his feet. The Kojiki records that this is 喪山 (the Mourning Mountain)* at the upper reaches of the 藍見河* in the land of Mino, and that the sword was named 大量*, also called 神度剣*.

As Ajisukitakahikone-no-Kami flew off in anger, his younger sister by the same mother, 高比売命*, composed a song to make her brother's name known. At the end of the song her brother's name is woven in: “阿治志貴多迦比古泥能迦微曾也”. The Kojiki records that this song is a 夷振* song.

A plain retelling of what the original text of the Kojiki says (no published translation has been used).

In the original text

即娶大國主神之女下照比賣，亦慮獲其國，至于八年不復奏

At once he married Shitateru-hime, the daughter of Ōkuninushi-no-Kami, plotted to obtain the land, and even after eight years he made no report.

或有邪心者，天若日子於此矢麻賀礼

If Ame-no-Wakahiko has an evil heart, let him be struck by this arrow and die (the words of 高木神* as he throws the arrow back).

故於今諺曰「雉之頓使」本，是也

What is called in today's proverb “the pheasant's one-way errand” (an envoy who goes and never comes back) originates here.

我者愛友故弔來耳，何吾比穢死人

I came only to mourn, because he was a dear friend. Why do you compare me to an unclean dead man? (the angry words of Ajisukitakahikone-no-Kami).

In the Nihon Shoki

Nihon Shoki, Book 2, section 9, main text

In the main text of section 9, because Ame-no-Hohi-no-Mikoto (天穂日命) made no report, his son 大背飯三熊之大人* was also sent, but he too followed his father and made no report. This deity does not appear in the Kojiki.

故仍遣其子大背飯三熊之大人
Nihon Shoki, Book 2, section 9, main text

In the main text of section 9, Ame-no-Wakahiko's (天稚彦) father 天国玉* carries the body up to heaven with a gale, and there builds a mourning house and mourns him. In the Kojiki, the father, wife and children come down to the earth and build the mourning house.

乃遣疾風擧尸致天。便造喪屋而殯之
Nihon Shoki, Book 2, section 9, variant account 6

In variant account 6 of section 9, the male pheasant sent first sees fields of millet and beans, alights there and does not return, and this is given as the origin of the expression “雉頓” (the pheasant's one-way errand). In the Kojiki, the expression “the pheasant's one-way errand” arises from the fact that Nakime, shot dead by Ame-no-Wakahiko, did not return.

因見粟田豆田。則留而不返。此世所謂雉頓之縁也
