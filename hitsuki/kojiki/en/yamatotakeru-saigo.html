The Death of Yamato Takeru (倭建命) and the White Bird

倭建命の最期と白鳥

Returning from the eastern provinces to Owari, Yamato Takeru (倭建命) left the Kusanagi sword (草那藝劔) with Miyazu-hime (美夜受比賣) and set out to seize the mountain deity of Ibuki (伊服岐) bare-handed; he was struck by a great icy rain, kept walking in his weakened body, and died at 能煩野*. Yamato Takeru became an eight-fathom white bird (八尋白智鳥) and flew away; his consorts and children followed it weeping and singing, and the White Bird Tomb (白鳥御陵) was made at 志幾* in Kawachi.

Key points to remember

  1. Main textLeaving the Kusanagi sword (草那藝劔) with Miyazu-hime (美夜受比賣), Yamato Takeru set out saying he would seize the mountain deity of Ibuki (伊服岐) bare-handed; because he made a declaration (言挙) that a white boar was the deity's messenger, he was struck and confounded by a great icy rain. A small-script note in the original text states that the white boar was in fact the deity itself.
  2. Main textAlong the route the weakened Yamato Takeru traveled, the origins of place names such as 居寤清泉, 當藝, 杖衝坂 and 三重* are told; at 能煩野* Yamato Takeru composed songs longing for his homeland (思國歌), and when he finished singing he died.
  3. Main textThe dead Yamato Takeru became an eight-fathom white bird (八尋白智鳥) and flew away; it is recorded that the four songs his consorts and children composed as they followed it, weeping, are still sung at the great funerals of emperors (大御葬).
  4. Main textThe white bird stopped at 志幾* in the province of Kawachi, and the White Bird Tomb (白鳥御陵) was made there, but the bird flew on from there, soaring through the sky.
  5. Compared with the Nihon ShokiIn the Nihon Shoki (the 40th year of Emperor Keikō, "this year"), the deity of 膽吹山* appears as a great serpent, and Yamato Takeru-no-Mikoto (日本武尊) dies at 能褒野* at the age of thirty. The white bird stops at 琴彈原 in Yamato and at 舊市邑 in Kawachi*, and the three tombs were all called White Bird Tombs (白鳥陵).
  6. CommentaryThe Kokugakuin University deity-name database (entry for Ibuki-no-Yama-no-Kami, 伊服岐能山之神) explains that on the question of why Yamato Takeru made a "mistaken" declaration (言挙) there are many arguments, such as theories that take up whether that deity was a target of the subjugation (言向) the emperor had ordered, or the content and spirit of the declaration, and that the debate is not settled. [source, Japanese]

The story

Yamato Takeru praised the old man who had continued his song at the 酒折宮* and gave him the post of governor of the eastern provinces (東國造). Crossing from Kai into Shinano (科野國), he subdued Shinano-no-Saka-no-Kami (科野之坂神), and returning to Owari he went to Miyazu-hime (美夜受比賣), to whom he had earlier made his promise. When Miyazu-hime offered the imperial meal and held up a cup of the imperial sake, there was menstrual blood on the hem of her 意須比*. Yamato Takeru saw it and composed a song, and Miyazu-hime answered with a song.

The two were united, and Yamato Takeru left his sword, the Kusanagi sword (草那藝劔), with Miyazu-hime and set out to seize the mountain deity of Ibuki (伊服岐). Saying, "I will seize the deity of this mountain directly, bare-handed," he climbed the mountain, and at its edge he met a white boar as large as an ox. Yamato Takeru made a declaration (言挙), saying, "This thing that has taken the form of a white boar is the deity's messenger. I will not kill it now; I will kill it on my way back," and climbed on, but a great icy rain fell and struck and confounded him. A small-script note in the original text records that the white boar was not the deity's messenger but the deity itself, and that he was confounded because he had made the declaration.

When he came down the mountain and rested at the clear spring of 玉倉部*, his mind cleared a little, so that spring is called 居寤清泉*. On reaching the 當藝野* plain he said, "My heart always meant to go soaring through the sky, but now my feet cannot walk and have become 當藝斯玖"*, so that place is called 當藝. Going on a little from there he was utterly exhausted and walked slowly leaning on a staff, so that place is called 杖衝坂*. When he reached the single pine at the cape of 尾津*, the sword he had left behind when he ate there before was still there and had not been lost, so Yamato Takeru composed a song.

On reaching the village of 三重* he said, "My legs feel bent threefold, and I am utterly exhausted," so that place is called 三重. On reaching 能煩野* he composed a song longing for his homeland, and continued with further songs. The original text calls these songs longing for the homeland (思國歌) and records the one that follows as a half-poem (片歌). When his illness suddenly grew grave, Yamato Takeru composed another song, and as soon as he finished singing it he died.

A fast-horse messenger was sent to the capital, and his consorts and children who were in Yamato came down, made a tomb, and crawled around the 那豆岐田* fields surrounding it, weeping and singing. Then Yamato Takeru became an eight-fathom white bird (八尋白智鳥), soared through the sky, and flew off toward the shore. His consorts and children followed weeping, forgetting the pain even when their feet were cut on the stubble of bamboo grass, and they composed songs as they waded into the sea and also when the bird alighted on the rocky shore.

These four songs were all sung at Yamato Takeru's funeral, and it is said that to this day they are sung at the great funerals of emperors (大御葬). The white bird flew from that province and stopped at 志幾* in the province of Kawachi, so a tomb was made there to settle it and was named the White Bird Tomb (白鳥御陵). But the bird flew on from there, soaring through the sky. While Yamato Takeru was going round pacifying the provinces, 七拳脛*, ancestor of the 久米直*, always served him as his cook (膳夫).

Yamato Takeru had six children in all: with 布多遲能伊理毘賣命* he had 帶中津日子命*, with 弟橘比賣命* he had 若建王*, and so on. Of these, 帶中津日子命 ruled the realm. 迦具漏比賣命*, granddaughter of 若建王, was taken as wife by Emperor Keikō and bore 大江王*, and 大中比賣命*, daughter of 大江王, was the mother of 香坂王* and Oshikuma-no-Miko (忍熊王). Emperor Keikō died at the age of 137, and his tomb is on the 山邊* road.

A plain retelling of what the original text of the Kojiki says (no published translation has been used).

In the original text

茲山神者。徒手直取而。

Yamato Takeru's words: "I will seize the deity of this mountain directly, bare-handed."

此化白猪者。非其神之使者。當其神之正身。

A small-script note in the original text: the thing that had taken the form of a white boar was not the deity's messenger but the deity itself.

於是化八尋白智鳥。翔天而向濱飛行。

Yamato Takeru became a great white bird, soared through the sky, and flew off toward the shore.

即號其御陵。謂白鳥御陵也。

The tomb made at 志幾* in Kawachi was named the White Bird Tomb (白鳥御陵).

In the Nihon Shoki

Nihon Shoki, Book 7 — the 40th year of Emperor Keikō, "this year" (是歳)

In the Nihon Shoki, the deity of 膽吹山* becomes a great serpent and blocks the road, and Yamato Takeru-no-Mikoto (日本武尊), thinking it was the messenger of the violent deity (荒神), stepped over it and went on. The mountain deity raised clouds and rained down ice, and Yamato Takeru-no-Mikoto at last recovered by drinking the water of a spring, so he named that spring 居醒泉*.

至膽吹山山神化大蛇當道。
Nihon Shoki, Book 7 — the 17th year of Emperor Keikō, spring, 3rd month (戊戌朔己酉)

Songs with the same words as the songs longing for the homeland (思國歌) that Yamato Takeru composes at 能煩野* in the Kojiki (such as the song beginning "夜麻登波。久爾能麻本呂婆") are, in the Nihon Shoki, songs that Emperor Keikō composed at 子湯縣 in Hyūga* longing for the capital, and they are called 思邦歌 (songs longing for the homeland).

是謂思邦歌也。
Nihon Shoki, Book 7 — the 40th year of Emperor Keikō, "this year" (是歳)

In the Nihon Shoki, Yamato Takeru-no-Mikoto dies at 能褒野* at the age of thirty and is buried in the 能褒野 tomb in Ise Province. He emerged from the tomb as a white bird and flew off toward Yamato, and only his clothes remained in the coffin. The white bird stopped at 琴彈原 in Yamato and at 舊市邑 in Kawachi*, and a tomb was made at each; the three tombs were called White Bird Tombs (白鳥陵).

故時人號是三陵曰白鳥陵。
